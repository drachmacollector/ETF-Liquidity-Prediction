# 1. The most important issues I found

## Issue 1 — NAV versus iNAV: the historical validation target needs to be reconciled

Critical

This is probably the most important methodological issue in the two documents.

Your technical specification defines the primary prediction target as:

\\[ Premium\_{i,t} = \frac{Close\_{i,t}-NAV\_{i,t}}{NAV\_{i,t}}\times 10,000 \\]

In other words, the model predicts the difference between the exchange closing price and the official end-of-day NAV.

That's a perfectly legitimate target for a daily forecasting system.

However, look at how the October 2025 event is described in the research document.

The reported 5–10% premiums were primarily reported as premiums over iNAV, not necessarily premiums over the official end-of-day NAV.

These are not interchangeable measurements.

Consider the following hypothetical example:

| Metric                 | Value |
| ---------------------- | ----- |
| ETF closing price      | ₹150  |
| Official NAV           | ₹145  |
| iNAV near market close | ₹148  |
| Premium vs NAV         | 3.45% |
| Premium vs iNAV        | 1.35% |

Both measurements are mathematically correct, but they describe different things.

This matters because your E1 validation explicitly asks whether the model would have identified the October 2025 event before the reported 5–10% premium episode.

If the historical event is defined using iNAV but your model predicts NAV deviation, you're evaluating two different quantities.

What I recommend:

Keep NAV as the primary target. It is the more practical choice given the availability of historical data.

However, modify the October 2025 event-study definition.

The model should answer:

Would the system have identified an unusually large closing-price-to-official-NAV deviation ahead of or during the October 2025 episode?

The reported iNAV premium should be retained as contextual evidence, not treated as the model's ground-truth target.

Also, explicitly acknowledge that the model cannot establish whether an observed closing-price/NAV deviation represents genuine arbitrage inefficiency or merely a valuation-timing discrepancy.

Your specification already acknowledges the NAV clock problem in Section 3.1. The missing piece is ensuring that the event-study success criteria use the same measurement as the model.

## Issue 2 — The circuit-lock definition is potentially incorrect

Critical

Technical Specification, Section 3.1:

> `lock(i,t)=1` if the day's close is within 1% of a ±20% move from the prior close.

I would change this.

The problem is that the applicable price-band reference is not necessarily the previous day's closing price.

Your research document explains several different historical mechanisms:

- The original ±20% framework referenced T-2 NAV.
- An interim T-1 reference was introduced for gold/silver ETFs in February 2026.
- The new framework became effective on September 7, 2026.
- The new commodity ETF framework uses dynamic bands beginning at ±6%, with staged relaxation.

Consequently, calculating circuit locks using a fixed ±20% movement from the previous close can misclassify historical observations.

It is especially problematic when you subsequently use these flags to exclude observations from headline metrics.

Recommended correction:

Define circuit-lock status using the actual applicable exchange price-band limits for the particular ETF, exchange and trading date.

If those historical limits cannot be reconstructed reliably, use a more conservative classification:

- Confirmed circuit-locked.
- Potentially constrained / near-band.
- Not identified as constrained.
- Unknown due to insufficient historical information.

Do not silently classify an observation as circuit-locked using an assumed ±20% threshold.

This does not mean you need to build the full SEBI price-band simulator now. You only need sufficiently accurate historical classification to prevent corrupted validation results.

## Issue 3 — The February 2026 interim regulatory regime needs more precise treatment

Major

Your technical specification defines three regimes:

| Regime | Period                        |
| ------ | ----------------------------- |
| R0     | Until 30 January 2026         |
| R1     | 1 February – 4 September 2026 |
| R2     | From 7 September 2026         |

This is a sensible starting point.

However, your research document specifically describes the February 1 intervention as a BSE circular changing the reference price for gold and silver ETFs to T-1 NAV.

The technical specification currently treats R1 as a uniform regime.

That potentially overlooks exchange-specific differences.

For example, an ETF traded on NSE and BSE may have different applicable historical reference-price treatment during the interim period.

Recommended correction:

Make the regulatory regime identifier dependent on:

- Exchange.
- ETF category.
- Trading date.
- Applicable exchange circular.

At minimum, maintain an exchange-specific regime history in the data layer.

You can still use a simplified R0/R1/R2 categorical feature for modelling, but the underlying historical classification must be accurate.

## Issue 4 — Your baseline models are not sufficiently defined for Task B

Major

This is a modelling consistency problem.

Section 7.1 specifies three baselines:

| Baseline                    | Task |
| --------------------------- | ---- |
| B0: Persistence             | A    |
| B1: Rolling-volatility rule | A/B  |
| B2: Peer-median model       | A/B  |

However, Task B is a probabilistic classification problem.

It predicts:

\\[ P(E\_{i,t+1}=1) \\]

A rule that simply flags an ETF when its current premium exceeds a threshold produces a binary output, not necessarily a calibrated probability.

Similarly, a peer-group rolling quantile can identify unusual conditions, but it doesn't automatically provide a meaningful probability of tomorrow's tail event.

This matters because your primary Task B metric is the Brier skill score.

You cannot fairly compare a calibrated LightGBM probability against a baseline that only outputs 0 or 1 without explicitly defining how that baseline is converted into a probability.

Recommended correction:

Require all Task B baselines to produce probability estimates.

For instance:

- Historical event frequency conditional on the current risk state.
- Rolling empirical tail-event frequency.
- Peer-group historical event frequency.

These can be relatively simple models. They don't need to be sophisticated.

The important requirement is that every model being evaluated against Brier score produces a probability on the same scale.

Also, B1's current formulation uses today's premium deviation to flag an event, whereas the target is tomorrow's event. Explicitly define how the current observation maps to a next-day prediction.

## Issue 5 — The project can miss persistently expensive ETFs

Major — conceptual issue

This is a subtler problem, but worth addressing.

Your Task B definition is:

\\[ E\_{i,t+1} = \mathbf{1}\left( |prem\_{i,t+1}-m60\_{i,t}| \geq \max(\tau,3MAD60\_{i,t}) \right) \\]

This measures whether tomorrow's premium deviates significantly from the ETF's own historical normal premium.

I understand why you've designed it this way: you don't want an ETF that normally trades at a small premium to constantly generate false alarms.

However, consider this situation:

- An ETF historically trades at a 0.5% premium.
- It starts trading at a persistent 5% premium.
- The premium remains around 5% for several months.
- The rolling 60-day median gradually adjusts to 5%.

Eventually, Task B may classify the ETF as normal, despite it continuing to trade at a substantial premium to NAV.

This is not necessarily a modelling error. It is a consequence of what you've chosen to predict.

Your two tasks therefore measure different things:

| Task                | What it identifies                                                                   |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| A: Premium interval | Absolute premium/discount level expected tomorrow                                    |
| B: Tail probability | Probability of an unusually large deviation from the ETF's own recent normal premium |

My recommendation: Keep the existing mathematical definition of Task B. It is defensible and useful.

But make sure the dashboard distinguishes between:

1. Absolute premium/discount level.
2. Probability of a new tail dislocation.

You could also introduce a simple descriptive absolute-premium warning threshold, independently of the ML risk classification.

That way, an ETF trading at a persistent 5% premium cannot appear entirely safe simply because its premium has become historically normal.

This is particularly relevant to the business use case: the sales team needs to know whether an ETF is expensive relative to NAV, not just whether its premium has recently become unusual.

## Issue 6 — Point-in-time integrity of the event calendar needs strengthening

Major

Your technical specification correctly establishes a point-in-time policy.

It also proposes an events table containing:

- Date.
- Event type.
- ETFs affected.
- Source URL.

However, this is insufficient for some of the events you're planning to use as features.

Consider an AMC announcing suspension of new FoF subscriptions.

There are potentially three different dates:

- The date the announcement was published.
- The date the suspension became effective.
- The date the event was subsequently recorded in your dataset.

These are not necessarily identical.

Suppose an announcement becomes effective on October 13, but the notice is published on October 14.

Your October 13 prediction must not use information from that announcement.

This is precisely the kind of leakage that your point-in-time methodology is designed to prevent.

Recommended correction:

Give event records at least two separate timestamps:

`effective_at` and `known_at`.

The latter represents when the information became publicly available.

For historical backtesting, features must use `known_at`, not simply the event's effective date.

This is especially important for FoF suspension notices, exchange circulars and corporate announcements.

Your research document already recognises the importance of `known_at` for revised NAV observations, so this would make the technical specification consistent with its own research methodology.&#x20;

## Issue 7 — Survivorship bias is recognised in the research document but not adequately enforced in the technical specification

Major

Your research document explicitly identifies survivorship as a data-quality concern:

> Include merged/closed/renamed schemes.

However, the technical specification's universe is primarily defined as all currently listed gold and silver ETFs with at least 250 trading days of history.

These are not equivalent.

If an ETF was delisted, merged or discontinued before November 2026, it could disappear from your current universe even though it was actively trading during the historical period you're backtesting.

That can distort the evaluation.

Recommended correction:

The historical ETF universe should include eligible ETFs that existed during the evaluation period, including subsequently merged, renamed or delisted ETFs wherever historical data are available.

Maintain inclusion and exclusion dates for each ETF.

You don't need an enormous historical ETF database. Given the relatively small universe, a manually maintained historical universe is perfectly reasonable.

The important thing is to avoid defining your historical sample solely from ETFs that survive today.

There is also one statistical point I would specifically flag.

### Conformal calibration and the small panel dataset

Your specification proposes conformalized quantile regression using a rolling calibration window of at least 250 ETF-days.

This is a reasonable starting approach.

However, your dataset is a panel: multiple ETFs are observed on the same trading dates, and their premiums are likely correlated.

Consequently, 250 ETF-day observations do not necessarily represent 250 independent observations.

Standard conformal coverage guarantees also require assumptions that may not hold in a time-dependent financial dataset.

I would retain CQR, but require the agent to:

- Evaluate empirical coverage separately across time periods and ETF groups.
- Monitor interval coverage deterioration after regime changes.
- Avoid presenting nominal 90% coverage as a mathematical guarantee under temporal dependence.
- Compare CQR against uncalibrated quantile regression.

This is important because calibration is one of the central selling points of your project. You should be particularly careful not to overstate it.

# 4. Things I would NOT change

There are several decisions in these documents that I think are correct and should remain intact.

First, the narrowed project scope.

Limiting the core universe to gold and silver ETFs, with a handful of equity ETFs as controls, is the right decision. It gives you enough room to investigate the underlying market mechanics without spreading your data engineering and modelling efforts across unrelated ETF categories.

Second, LightGBM instead of immediately pursuing LSTMs or Transformers.

This is a sensible modelling decision. You have relatively little historical data, rare tail events, a small cross-sectional universe and predominantly tabular features. Deep learning would introduce complexity without an established justification.

Third, the emphasis on baselines.

The requirement that LightGBM must outperform simple alternatives is essential. A technically sophisticated model that cannot outperform persistence or rolling statistical thresholds is not a successful demonstration.

Fourth, the two separate prediction tasks.

The premium interval and tail-event probability provide complementary information. Keeping them separate is better than trying to compress everything into a single arbitrary risk score.

Fifth, the decision to park the SEBI band simulator.

I agree with this. The new regulatory framework is important context, but implementing its entire state machine would substantially increase scope. Your research document can preserve that work for a later extension.

Sixth, the decision to use a local Streamlit dashboard.

For a research demonstration and internship presentation, this is entirely adequate. You don't need React, a backend API, authentication, a database server or production deployment.

Seventh, the pre-registration requirement.

Freezing event definitions, thresholds and success criteria before examining final test results is particularly valuable. It makes the demonstration much more credible.

## 7. One thing I would deliberately leave for the development roadmap

Do not try to solve every unresolved technical question inside the Master Specification.

For example, you don't need to decide right now:

- The exact LightGBM hyperparameters.
- The final feature-selection methodology.
- The exact number of rolling training windows.
- Every individual API or data-download implementation.
- The final Streamlit layout.
- The precise Python module structure.
- The final calibration-window size.
- Every edge case in the data-cleaning pipeline.

Those belong in the individual phase specifications.

Your technical document should establish the constraints and expected outcomes. The phase documents should establish the implementation details.

That distinction is important because otherwise you'll end up with a Master Specification that becomes unnecessarily enormous and difficult to maintain.

## 9. What should happen immediately afterwards?

## Development planning workflow

1. Freeze the two master documents

   Research Document = authoritative research and business context.

   Technical Master Specification = authoritative scope and engineering requirements.
2. Generate the complete development roadmap

   One highly detailed Markdown document per phase, with implementation instructions, testing, acceptance criteria and handoff requirements.

Once the master documents are corrected, you have a sufficiently mature foundation to begin generating the development roadmap.

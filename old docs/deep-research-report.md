# Project Research Document

**Indian ETF Market Overview:** Exchange-traded funds (ETFs) in India have grown substantially over the past decade, with a wide range of equity, fixed-income, commodity (gold, silver) and international products.  By late 2026 there were dozens of ETFs listed on NSE and BSE, tracking indices from Nifty-50 and Sensex to sectoral and thematic baskets (e.g. manufacturing, internet, consumption, IT, banking, midcap/smallcap) and assets (gold, silver, U.S. indices).  ETFs combine features of mutual funds and stocks: they pool investor money like a fund but trade intraday on exchanges.  The onus is on market makers/authorized participants (APs) to arbitrage away price deviations and maintain liquidity.  For example, Mirae’s own Nifty50 ETF website notes that “active authorized participants” help keep its trading price close to the indicative NAV (iNAV).  The Indian ETF AUM is still only a small fraction of total mutual fund AUM, but growth has accelerated; regulators note that ETF inflows and products are poised to grow, even as the industry faced the first year in a decade (2023) where ETF AUM growth lagged mutual fund growth.   Domestic investors increasingly favor ETFs for diversification and liquidity, and active distributors (bank channels, RIAs, brokers) are targeting ETF sales to new retail and institutional segments.

Key products include Mirae Asset Nifty 50 ETF (large-cap), Nifty Bank ETF, Nifty IT ETF, thematic “India Internet”, “Manufacturing”, “New Age Consumption” ETFs, plus gold and silver ETFs and even global equity ETFs (Hang Seng Tech, Nasdaq-100).  Mirae’s product team is led by senior fund managers (e.g. Sudip Shere, Ritesh Patel on Nifty50 ETF) and an ETF product group; distribution is led by Umesh Kumar D. (National Head, ETF Sales). **Regulatory and Market Mechanics:** In India, ETFs are governed largely by SEBI’s mutual fund regulations and exchange trading rules.  By design, each ETF must have a single benchmark/index it closely replicates.  Authorized Participants (APs), usually large brokerages/market-makers, can create or redeem ETF units directly with the fund house in large blocks (e.g. 50,000 units for Mirae’s Nifty50 ETF). These APs purchase or sell the underlying basket of stocks or assets, and transact at an intraday NAV (i.e. the “true” fund value at the time).  SEBI explicitly allows ETFs’ APs and large investors to transact at an intra-day NAV equal to the executed price of the underlying securities.  This mechanism is meant to keep the ETF market price tethered to its NAV through arbitrage.  

Since 2024–2026, regulators have introduced major changes to ETF trading.  Historically, stock exchanges used a fixed **price band** of ±20% around a base price set by the NAV from two days prior (T-2 NAV).  In mid-2026, SEBI announced a shift to **dynamic price bands** and new auction mechanisms to improve price discovery. Equity and debt ETFs will now open trading with a reference price equal to the previous trading day’s volume-weighted average closing price, not the stale T-2 NAV.  Price bands will start at ±10% (equity/debt ETFs) or ±6% (commodity ETFs like gold/silver) from that reference, and can expand by 3% increments after cooling-off periods if those limits are hit.  Commodity ETFs (gold, silver) will also have a **pre-open call auction** to incorporate overnight global price moves.  These rules (effective late 2026, with full phase-in by 2027) aim to keep ETF prices aligned with underlying values and global markets. All exchanges (NSE, BSE, others) will enforce the same bands and auctions.  (For context, under current rules the circuit filters could open at ±20% but rarely expand; the new regime is much more dynamic.)  Other key regulations include: ETFs must closely track their index (indexed scheme rules) and may hold minimal cash; gold ETFs must hold physical bullion in SEBI-approved vaults tracking LBMA gold prices; and foreign ETFs face strict limits (no new mutual fund flows after RBI’s $1B cap was hit in 2024).  Even market microstructure changes like optional T+0 settlement (intra-day clearing) are being discussed, which could further reduce latency in ETF trades (Sebi outlined T+0 beta guidelines in 2024).  

**Data Landscape:** Building an AI-driven ETF system requires rich data inputs. Key data sources include:  
- **Exchange Market Data:** NSE and BSE publish real-time trade data (prices, volumes, bid/ask) for all listed ETFs. Historical price/volume time-series (“bhavcopy” or downloadable CSV) can be obtained via the exchanges’ websites or data feeds (e.g. NSE’s Historical Data portal).   In practice, accessing high-frequency (tick or minute) data may require paid APIs or vendor feeds, but end-of-day data is readily available from NSE/BSE public archives.  
- **Indicative NAVs (iNAV):** Exchanges and data vendors disseminate each ETF’s iNAV intraday (typically updated ~every 15 seconds during trading). For example, AMFI publishes official NAV (end-of-day) and some broker portals (or AMC websites) provide iNAV streams. Note: iNAV is only indicative (non-executable) and may lag or be stale for illiquid international ETFs.  
- **AMFI/Mutual Fund Data:** The Association of Mutual Funds in India (AMFI) website publishes daily NAVs for all mutual fund schemes including ETFs (with a one-day lag). AMFI also releases monthly AUM and flow reports by fund category. This provides a source for historical ETF NAV time series, portfolio compositions (through Scheme Information Documents), and aggregated net flows.  
- **Index Data:** Underlying indices (Nifty, BSE indices, sectoral indices, global indices) can be obtained from official index providers or exchanges. For instance, NSE publishes daily index values (Nifty 50, Bank Nifty, etc) and constituents. These are needed to compute expected ETF performance and tracking error.  
- **Macroeconomic and Market Indicators:** Relevant inputs include currency (USD/INR) rates, commodity prices (domestic gold/silver, global spot prices), interest rates and yield curves (for bond ETFs), and sector performance. RBI reference rates (for converting gold prices) and economic data (GDP, inflation) can enrich forecasting.  
- **Alternative Data:** News and social media sentiment (financial news, Twitter/X) may influence ETF demand (e.g. investor interest in certain sectors). Third-party providers (news APIs, Twitter data feeds) can be tapped. Additionally, data on institutional flows (like FPI activity in stocks) could serve as proxy signals.  
- **Limitations:** Many data sources have constraints. Exchange-provided data may require authentication or subscription for granular access; AMFI data is daily-only and in PDF/CSV formats; free feeds may not cover all ETFs (especially international listings). Historical iNAV snapshots are not archived publicly. Index constituent weights change quarterly, so rebalance data must be tracked. Data quality (e.g. missing values for illiquid ETFs) will be a challenge to handle.  

**Industry Landscape and Competitors:** In India, most ETF analytics are manual or rely on generic tools. Brokers and research firms provide basic ETF info (performance charts, risk metrics) but lack predictive analytics. Globally, some platforms (e.g. Bloomberg, Morningstar) offer advanced ETF analytics, but these are often expensive and not India-specific. A few fintech startups (e.g. FundSageAI) are emerging, but they focus on mutual funds and portfolio review, not ETF microstructure. FundSageAI, for instance, markets itself as “an AI-powered mutual fund analytics platform” for Indian investors.  There are also “AI ETF” products (mostly foreign ETFs managed by algorithms), but not tools.  No leading Indian solution offers real-time premium/discount alerts, smart arbitrage signals or predictive flows. This gap highlights an opportunity: an AI-driven dashboard could combine price, volume, NAV, and market data to flag trading opportunities or risk (e.g. an ETF about to spike in premium), far beyond what current ETF screeners do.

# Technical Master Specification

**System Overview:** The proposed system is an end-to-end AI-powered ETF analytics platform. It will ingest market data (exchange trades, NAVs, index values, news), process and store it, generate predictive analytics, and present the results through APIs and a user interface. Key components include a **Data Pipeline** (to fetch, clean and store data), a **Feature Engineering Module** (to compute derived metrics like premium/discount, momentum, volatility), one or more **Machine Learning Engines** (for forecasting and anomaly detection), and presentation layers (APIs and dashboard). The architecture will be modular and scalable, using robust technologies (e.g. Python, SQL/NoSQL databases, containerization) so that each part can be developed, tested, and deployed independently.  

**Data Pipeline and Storage:**  Data ingestion will be automated via scheduled jobs. For example, a Python/ETL script or workflow (e.g. Apache Airflow) will download end-of-day ETF prices and volumes from NSE/BSE, and daily NAVs from AMFI. Intraday data and iNAVs may come from a real-time feed or web-scraping (exchange APIs or vendor APIs if licensed). Exchange-traded data can be stored in a time-series optimized database (e.g. kdb+/ClickHouse or PostgreSQL with TimescaleDB) to efficiently query historical prices. NAV and fundamental data can go to a relational database (PostgreSQL). Metadata (ETF constituents, index composition, rebalance dates) will be stored in auxiliary tables.  All raw data will be logged and timestamped for audit and backtesting. The pipeline must handle irregularities: e.g. if an ETF was not traded on a given day, carry forward the last price or mark as missing. 

**Feature Engineering:**  Once raw data is stored, a feature engineering module will compute inputs for the AI models. This includes: 
- **Price-based features:** daily returns, moving averages (short/long), volatility (e.g. 10-day std), bid-ask spreads (if available), and ETF-specific metrics (premium/discount = (Price - iNAV)/iNAV). Since iNAV updates ~every 15 seconds, we can track minute-level premium statistics (max/min discount) during each day.  
- **Index-based features:** correlation or tracking error with the underlying index, index momentum, sector rotation signals (e.g. how many sectors are in uptrend, using index sector ETF returns).  
- **Order book / volume features:** intraday volume spikes (e.g. sudden high volume can precede price moves).  
- **Fundamental features:** AUM, flows (if monthly data available), expense ratios.  
- **Sentiment/NLP features:** textual analysis of news headlines or social media keywords related to the ETF’s sector or holdings (e.g. count of bullish news articles on IT stocks for a tech ETF). This may use pre-trained language models or sentiment analysis APIs.  
- **Regulatory context features:** flags for market conditions (e.g. volatility index level, whether a dynamic band expansion occurred recently, time-to-rebalance).  
All features will be stored in a clean, time-aligned format (one row per ETF per time-step), along with metadata (ETF identifier, asset class, etc.).  

**AI/ML Models:**  The core intelligence will come from predictive models. Likely model types include: 
- **Time-Series Forecasting (Regression):** For example, an LSTM or transformer-based neural network could forecast the next day’s premium/discount or relative price movement for each ETF using historical price and feature data. This addresses questions like “Will ETF X trade at a premium tomorrow?”. Alternative methods like ARIMA or Random Forest regression on lagged features will be evaluated.  
- **Classification Models:** We may frame it as a classification (e.g. “next session premium > 2%?”) to simplify some decisions. In that case, decision trees or gradient-boosted models (XGBoost/LightGBM) could predict discrete signals (buy/sell/hold). 
- **Anomaly/Signal Detection:** Unsupervised models (clustering, density-estimation) could flag unusual market conditions (e.g. an ETF suddenly deviating from historical correlations). 
- **Reinforcement Learning (Advanced):** A possible extension is to train an RL agent to propose trading actions in a simulated market environment, though this is more experimental.  
Models will be chosen and tuned based on validation performance and business interpretability. We will justify model choice by experimentation (e.g. if LSTM outperforms linear regression on validation, we document that).  

**Model Training and Evaluation:**  The training workflow will use historical data with a time-series cross-validation (rolling windows). For example, use data up to Dec 2023 to train models, validate on Q1 2024, etc. Key evaluation metrics include Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) or Root MSE for regression (premium forecasts) and accuracy/AUC for classification signals. However, since financial predictions have economic impact, we will also use **business metrics**: for instance, a backtested trading strategy based on model signals will be simulated (see next section). Models will be retrained periodically as new data accrues. During training, data splitting will respect chronology (no peeking into future), and we will check for overfitting by comparing train vs validation errors. 

**Backtesting Framework:**  A critical component is a backtesting engine. Given historical prices, the system will simulate the application of the model’s output. For example, if the model predicts an ETF will trade at a discount tomorrow, the backtester might “buy” at that moment and “sell” at a later time (or at NAV redemption). This will measure hypothetical P&L and Sharpe ratio of using the AI signals. We will define clear rules (entry/exit conditions, position sizing, transaction costs) to evaluate realistic returns. The results will feed back into model iteration. All backtesting code will be version-controlled and use the stored historical data; key backtest results (profit curve, drawdown) will be plotted for analysis. 

**Backend and API Design:**  The system’s core logic (feature computation, model inference) will run on servers (likely in Python). A RESTful API (e.g. using FastAPI or Flask) will expose endpoints for accessing predictions and analytics. For instance, an endpoint `/predict?etf=XYZ` could return next-day premium forecasts, or `/report/etf/XYZ` could return feature values and model confidence. The backend will query the database for the latest input features, run the ML model, and format results in JSON. Batch API jobs (cron) could compute signals overnight and store them in the DB. We will secure the API with authentication tokens so that only authorized Mirae users or services can call it. The backend logic will be containerized (Docker) for deployment. 

**Frontend and Visualization:**  A web-based dashboard will provide visual access. Built with a modern JavaScript framework (React or Angular), it will display key information: interactive charts of ETF price vs iNAV over time, current premium/discount, and AI prediction overlays. Users can select ETFs or indices, view model predictions and confidence intervals, and drill into details (e.g. feature contributions). The UI will highlight alerts (e.g. “Gold ETF nearing upper band”) and allow downloading reports. For product teams, a section might list trending sectors or “investment ideas” based on model outputs. The design will prioritize clarity: for example, a chart panel could show price and iNAV lines (to visually confirm closeness), alongside an indicator of model-predicted direction. All frontend content will be fed by the API. 

**Deployment, Monitoring, and Maintenance:**  The system will be deployed on reliable cloud infrastructure (AWS, Azure or similar). We will use container orchestration (Kubernetes or ECS) to manage services. CI/CD pipelines (using GitHub Actions or Jenkins) will automate testing and deployment of new code. Monitoring tools (like Prometheus/Grafana) will track system health (latency, error rates) and model performance drift (e.g. distribution of prediction errors over time). Alerts will be set up so engineers know if e.g. data feed fails or model MAPE suddenly increases. All software components will have logs. We will also implement a data quality check (e.g. flag missing values from NSE feed). Retraining can be automated on a schedule or when performance degrades. A small team lead (or DevOps engineer) will oversee maintenance and updates. 

**Core Features and Success Criteria:**  The finished application will offer: (1) **Real-time Monitoring of ETFs:** Live dashboards of ETF prices, iNAV, spreads, liquidity metrics; (2) **Predictive Alerts:** Daily or intraday signals highlighting ETFs likely to move or deviate from fair value, with confidence scores; (3) **Analytical Tools:** Charts and reports for portfolio risk (e.g. sector exposures, tracking errors) and what-if analysis; (4) **Advisory Output:** Suggested strategies for sales (e.g. identify large client orders for creation/redemption, or new fund proposals). Deliverables include the data pipeline code, trained model artifacts, API endpoints, and the front-end dashboard. Success will be measured by model metrics (e.g. achieving a specified error or signal accuracy on test data), and ultimately by user uptake – for example, whether the ETF sales team uses the alerts in client pitches. Evaluation metrics will cover both technical (prediction error, backtest ROI) and business (increased ETF AUM or trade volumes referenced to model signals). All components will be documented so that development can continue after this phase. 

**Technology Choices:** Python will be the primary language (pandas, scikit-learn, TensorFlow/PyTorch for ML). We will use PostgreSQL (with TimescaleDB extension) for time-series data. The backend API might use FastAPI for speed. The frontend might use React. Docker containers will package services. If needed, we may integrate cloud ML services (e.g. AWS SageMaker) for scalable training. Each choice is driven by practicality (broad community support, Mirae’s existing tech stack) and performance considerations.  

In summary, the system will concretely deliver predictive analytics and data engineering specifically tailored to Indian ETFs. It will respect all regulatory constraints (e.g. incorporate the dynamic band rules into signals) and provide actionable intelligence for Mirae’s ETF business. The blueprint above gives developers a roadmap to implement each module end-to-end, from raw data ingestion to the final user interface. All designs focus on **real-world utility** for ETF sales and product teams, as opposed to purely academic modeling: for instance, the model output is integrated into a workflow (dashboards, APIs) that fits the sales team’s needs.
